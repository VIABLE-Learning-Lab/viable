# From a topic to a research question

*Example College Library research guide. © 2026 VIABLE Lab, CC BY 4.0.*

A topic is what you are interested in ("social media"). A research question is what you want to find out about it ("How does using social media late at night affect high school students' sleep?"). Searching goes much better with a question.

## Narrow the topic

Ask yourself who, where, when and what about your topic:

- **Who?** Teenagers, older adults, nurses, first-year college students.
- **Where?** One country, cities or rural areas, schools, workplaces.
- **When?** The last ten years, during a particular event, a historical period.
- **What about it?** Causes, effects, costs, how it has changed, how people feel about it.

Combine two or three of these: "the effect of late-night phone use on sleep among high school students in the United States".

## Turn it into a question

Good research questions start with **how** or **why**, or compare two things. They can't be answered with yes or no, or with one fact you could look up. Compare:

- Too broad: "What is the impact of technology?"
- Too narrow: "How many hours did teenagers sleep in 2019?"
- About right: "How does using a phone after 10 p.m. relate to how long high school students sleep?"

## Pick your search words

Underline the key ideas in your question, then list other words for each one. For the question above:

| Idea | Other words |
| --- | --- |
| phone use | smartphone, screen time, social media, mobile device |
| sleep | sleep duration, sleep quality, insomnia, bedtime |
| high school students | adolescents, teenagers, teens, youth |

Search with one word from each row, and swap words when you get too few or too many results.

## When to ask a librarian

If your first searches find nothing, or thousands of results that don't fit, your question may need adjusting. A librarian can help you narrow or widen it in a few minutes.
